# Video 001 — Brief

## Working topic

**Why Your Brain Replays Embarrassing Moments at Night**

## Core idea

A short, relatable psychology-style video about why awkward or embarrassing memories can suddenly feel vivid when someone is lying in bed at night with fewer distractions.

The video should begin with a highly recognizable moment, explain the experience in simple language, and end with a satisfying takeaway rather than sounding clinical or overly academic.

## Target

- Language: English
- Format: faceless YouTube video
- Duration: ~60 seconds
- Spoken length target: ~130–160 words
- Audience: general English-speaking audience
- Tone: conversational, clever, relatable, slightly dramatic
- Visual style: minimal illustrated scenes with strong visual metaphors
- Editing style: frequent visual changes, but driven by visual beats rather than one image per sentence

## Viewer promise

By the end of the video, the viewer should understand **why old embarrassing memories can become louder at night** and feel that the explanation matches something they have personally experienced.

## Suggested narrative shape

1. **Hook:** A very specific late-night experience: you are about to sleep, then your brain suddenly remembers something embarrassing from years ago.
2. **Recognition:** Show how strangely vivid and urgent the memory feels.
3. **Explanation:** At night there are fewer external distractions, so internally generated thoughts and memories can capture more attention.
4. **Amplification:** Fatigue and rumination can make the memory feel more emotionally important than it actually is.
5. **Payoff:** The memory is not necessarily resurfacing because it matters now; your brain simply has fewer competing signals.
6. **Ending:** A concise, memorable line that reframes the experience.

## Content rules for Video 001

- Do not invent scientific claims merely to make the script sound authoritative.
- Avoid fake statistics and vague phrases like “studies prove” unless we actually verify a source.
- Prefer everyday explanation over neuroscience jargon.
- Do not diagnose the viewer or frame normal late-night rumination as a disorder.
- Keep sentences easy for natural ElevenLabs narration.
- Write visually: important ideas should be easy to convert into clear illustrated scenes.
- Avoid a generic “Did you know?” opening.

## Visual opportunities

Potential imagery to explore later:

- character trying to fall asleep
- a tiny old memory suddenly appearing
- memory expanding into a giant projection above the bed
- an empty/dark room with fewer distractions
- spotlight metaphor: one embarrassing memory under a bright spotlight
- exhausted character arguing with their own brain
- old awkward scene replaying like a movie
- the same memory becoming small again in daylight

These are directions, not final visual beats.

## Open questions

To resolve during scripting/research:

- How much scientific explanation should fit into 60 seconds?
- Should we explicitly name concepts such as rumination, negativity bias, or reduced external stimulation?
- What is the strongest final takeaway without overstating the psychology?
